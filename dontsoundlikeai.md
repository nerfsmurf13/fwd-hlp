# Signs of AI Writing

This is a list of writing and formatting conventions typical of AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, with real examples taken from articles, drafts, and online content. It serves as a guide to help detect undisclosed AI-generated content.

Not all text featuring these indicators is AI-generated, as the large language models (LLMs) that power AI chatbots are trained on human writing. Many elements of AI writing can also be found in human editorials, blogs, or creative writing. Moreover, this list is descriptive, not prescriptive; it consists of observations, not strict rules.

The patterns detailed here are only potential signs of a problem. Minor structural quirks—such as excessive boldface, broken markup, or unusual citation styles—can often point to less outwardly visible issues regarding depth and factual accuracy.

---

## General Caveats

### AI Detection Tools

Do not solely rely on artificial intelligence content detection tools. While they perform better than random chance, these tools have non-trivial error rates. Detectors are highly susceptible to factors such as minor text modifications (e.g., paraphrasing, markup edits, and spacing changes) and the use of language models not seen during the detector's training phase.

### Detection Limitations

Do not rely entirely on personal intuition. Research shows that even heavy users of LLMs correctly determine whether an article was generated by AI only about 90% of the time. This means an expert user tagging ten pages as AI-generated will likely make at least one false positive. People who do not use LLMs frequently typically perform only slightly better than random chance.

Writers may also adjust their behavior to avoid accusations of AI use, or they may become defensive when confronted with common AI tropes.

## Content and Style Indicators

### Statistical Regression to the Mean

LLMs use statistical algorithms to infer what word should come next based on a massive corpus of training material. Because of this, their output tends to regress to the mean—meaning the text leans toward the most statistically likely result that applies to the widest variety of cases.

For example, because LLMs are trained on vast internet datasets where famous figures are described with positive, important-sounding language, the model will often omit specific, unusual, or nuanced facts (which are statistically rare) and replace them with generic, positive descriptions (which are statistically common). A highly specific phrase like *"inventor of the first train-coupling device"* might be smoothed over by an AI into *"a revolutionary titan of industry"*. The subject becomes simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated.

### Undue Emphasis on Significance, Legacy, and Trends

> **Words to Watch:** *stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a vital/significant/crucial/pivotal/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted*

LLM writing frequently puffs up the importance of a subject by adding grand statements about how arbitrary aspects of the topic contribute to a broader trend or historical legacy. There is a distinct, easily identifiable repertoire of ways it constructs these statements.

#### Real-World Examples

* **Example (Institutional History):**
> "The Statistical Institute of Catalonia was officially established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics in Spain. [...] The founding of Idescat represented a significant shift toward regional statistical independence, enabling Catalonia to develop a statistical system tailored to its unique socio-economic context. This initiative was part of a broader movement across Spain to decentralize administrative functions and enhance regional governance."


* **Example (Geography):**
> "Kumba has long been an important center for trade and agriculture. [...] The establishment of road networks connecting Kumba to other parts of the Southwest Region, such as Mamfe and Buea, helped solidify its role as a regional hub."



LLMs include these high-level statements for even the most mundane subjects like population data or basic etymology. When discussing biology, LLMs tend to over-emphasize connections to a broad ecosystem or environment, even when those connections are generic or tenuous. They also frequently belabor a species' conservation status and ongoing preservation efforts, even if the status is officially unknown.

#### Real-World Examples (Biology)

* **Example 1:**
> "Currently, there is no specific conservation assessment for Lethrinops lethrinus by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN). However, the general health of the Lake Malawi ecosystem is crucial for the survival of this and other endemic species. Factors such as overfishing, pollution, and habitat destruction could potentially impact their populations."


* **Example 2:**
> "It plays a role in the ecosystem and contributes to Hawaii's rich cultural heritage. [...] Preserving this endemic species is vital not only for ecological diversity but also for sustaining the cultural traditions connected to Hawaii’s native flora."



### Canned Emphasis on Notability and Media Coverage

> **Words to Watch:** *independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, profiled in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence*

LLMs frequently attempt to establish importance by explicitly listing the *types* of media sources a subject has been covered in (e.g., trade publications, regional media, prominent outlets), often pairing this with inaccurate or superficial summaries of what those sources actually said.

#### Real-World Examples

* **Example 1:**
> "She spoke about AI on CNN, and was featured in Vogue, Wired, Toronto Star, and other media. [...] Her insights have also been featured in Wired, Refinery29, and other prominent media outlets."


* **Example 2:**
> "Its significance is documented in archived school event programs and regional press coverage, including the Mesabi Daily News, which regularly reviewed performances held there."


* **Example 3:**
> "The restaurant has also been mentioned in ABC News coverage relating to incidents in the surrounding precinct, underscoring its role as a well-known late-night venue in the city."



When writing about individuals or modern entities, LLMs will also regularly insert variants of the phrase *"maintains an active social media presence"*, a phrasing choice that remains highly idiosyncratic to machine text.

### Superficial Analyses

> **Words to Watch:** *highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing..., ensuring..., reflecting/symbolizing..., contributing to..., cultivating/fostering..., encompassing..., valuable insights, align/resonate with*

AI chatbots tend to insert empty, superficial analyses of information. This is often achieved by attaching a present participle (`-ing`) phrase to the very end of sentences, or attributing vague opinions to anonymous third parties.

#### Real-World Examples

* **Example 1:**
> "As of the April 2008 census, the population of Douera stood at approximately 56,998 inhabitants, creating a lively community within its borders. Situated in the central-north region of the country, Douera enjoys close proximity to the capital city, Algiers, further enhancing its significance as a dynamic hub of activity and culture."


* **Example 2:**
> "Over the years, Darbhanga Junction has seen several upgrades and modernization efforts aimed at improving facilities and operational efficiency, reflecting its continued relevance in the regional and national transportation landscape."


* **Example 3:**
> "The phenomenon has generated debate about authenticity, consent, and the psychological effects of digitally extending personhood. [...] Collectively, these works have shaped emerging policy discussions about ownership, consent, and dignity in digital resurrection technologies."

## Language and Grammar Patterns

AI-generated text displays consistent patterns in syntax, word choice, and sentence construction that human writing rarely displays to the same density.

### High Density of "AI Vocabulary"

Many linguistic studies show that LLMs vastly overuse a specific core group of vocabulary words. While any of these words can appear naturally in human writing, a high density of them co-occurring in a short text is a strong signature of AI generation.

The distribution of these words shifts depending on the specific model used and its training era:

* **Early Era (e.g., GPT-4 baseline):** *Additionally* (especially beginning a sentence), *boasts* (meaning "has"), *bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape* (as an abstract noun), *meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry* (as an abstract noun), *testament, valuable, vibrant.*
* **Mid Era (e.g., GPT-4o):** *Align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.*
* **Later Era (e.g., GPT-5 generations):** *Emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing*, alongside heavy repetition of media-attribution phrases.

### Avoidance of Basic Copulatives ("Is" and "Are")

> **Words to Watch:** *serves as, stands as, marks, represents, features, maintains, offers*

LLM-generated text frequently replaces simple verbs like *is*, *are*, or *has* with more complex or marketing-oriented phrasal alternatives. For example, instead of writing *"The building has four rooms,"* an AI will regularly write *"The building features four separate spaces"* or *"serves as an exhibition space."*

Studies of academic abstracts modified by AI show a significant net decrease in the usage of basic copulas (*is*/*are*) alongside a sharp spike in descriptive, promotional verbs.

### Negative Parallelisms

AI models frequently structure descriptions as if they are actively clearing up a common misconception, or assuming the reader has arrived at an incomplete conclusion. This creates a distinct contrasting pattern.

* **"Not just X, but also Y":**
> "Self-Portrait by Yayoi Kusama, executed in 2010 and currently preserved in the famous Uffizi Gallery in Florence, constitutes not only a work of self-representation, but a visual document of her obsessions, visual strategies and psychobiographical narratives."


* **"Not X, but Y":**
> "This dispersal is not dissolution. Rather, it constitutes what Deleuze might describe as 'becoming'—an identity in flux, constituted through iterative difference. Through this lens, Kusama’s self-portrait is not a mirror but a portal: not a representation of self, but a mechanism for its constant reinvention."



### Rule of Three

LLMs display a notable reliance on the "rule of three" to make their superficial analyses sound structured and authoritative. This manifests as lists of three consecutive adjectives, or sentences concluded by three distinct, parallel short phrases (e.g., *"visually appealing, engaging, and entertaining"*).

### Lexical Diversity and Elegant Variation

Generative AI models operate with internal repetition penalties designed to stop the model from using the exact same word twice in close succession. This forces the AI to cycle through synonyms, creating an artificial style known as "elegant variation." If a text repeatedly cycles through terms like *constraints*, *boundaries*, *confines*, and *limitations* within two paragraphs to avoid repeating a single word, it often points to machine generation.

## Formatting and Structural Quirks

### Title Case in Subheadings

AI chatbots strongly lean toward capitalizing every main word in structural section headings (Title Case), whereas modern digital style guides typically favor sentence-case capitalization.

### Overuse of Boldface

AI models frequently use boldface to emphasize terms in a highly mechanical, repetitive fashion—a habit inherited from formatting styles found in technical readmes, sales pitches, and slide decks.

### Inline-Header Vertical Lists

AI output regularly defaults to a highly rigid vertical list format where every single bullet point or numbered item begins with an inline boldface header followed immediately by a colon, like this:

* **Historical Context:** Text goes here...
* **Technical Details:** Text goes here...

### Overuse of Em Dashes

While human writers use em dashes (`—`) selectively, LLM output uses them with a much higher density to create a "punched up," rhythmic style, often replacing standard commas, colons, or parentheses.

### Curly Quotation Marks and Apostrophes

Many dominant AI engines (such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek models) default to using typographical curly quotation marks (`“...”`) and curly apostrophes (`’`) in raw text output, whereas standard web inputs typically default to straight quotes (`"..."`).

## Technical Artifacts and Bugs

When AI-generated text is copied directly from a chatbot user interface, unique technical footprints and syntax errors often cross over into the clipboard.

### Raw Markdown Syntax

Chatbots write in Markdown natively. When an inexperienced user pastes AI text directly into platforms that do not support Markdown, the formatting code leaks into public view:

* Using hashes (`## Headings`) for section titles.
* Using asterisks (`bold`) or underscores (`_italics_`) for emphasis.
* Leaving raw Markdown link syntax in plain view: `[Text](URL)`.

### Reference and Search Engine Leakage

When an AI model uses internal search tools or browsing extensions to answer a prompt, unique system citation tags can accidentally render as plain text if the user copies the output hastily. Watch out for these specific programmatic bugs:

* **PUA Unicode Strings:** Strings like `citeturn0search0` or `2`.
* **ChatGPT Internal References:** Strings like `:contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}` or `[oai_citation:1‡website.com]`.
* **Perplexity Tags:** Inline formatting tags like `[attached_file:1]` or `[web:1]`.
* **Grok Citation Metadata:** JSON blocks or tags like `<grok-card>` or `[](grok_render_citation_card_json={"cardIds":["..."]})`.
* **Lenticular Brackets:** Technical inline search pointers like `【85†L261-269】`.
* **Attribution Metadata:** Appended JSON arrays such as `({"attribution":{"attributableIndex":"1009-1"}})`.

### Knowledge-Cutoff Disclaimers

When an AI model lacks data due to its training limits or failed search queries, it often explicitly alerts the user with phrasal templates:

* *"As of my last knowledge update in..."*
* *"While specific information is limited in the available search results, the subject likely..."*
* *"Because details are not extensively documented in readily available sources, they maintain a low profile..."*

### UTM Tracking Parameters appended to URLs

When ChatGPT, Copilot, or Grok surf the web to gather sources, they often automatically append tracking parameters to the links they generate. The presence of the following strings within a text's source links confirms AI retrieval:

* `utm_source=openai`
* `utm_source=chatgpt.com`
* `utm_source=copilot.com`
* `referrer=grok.com`

## Signs of Legitimate Human Writing

To avoid false accusations, it is critical to look for counter-indicators that strongly align with human creation.

* **Age of the Text:** If the content was published or logged anywhere prior to the public launch of ChatGPT (November 30, 2022), AI involvement can be entirely ruled out.
* **Ability to Explain Editorial Choices:** When a human makes an error, a typo, or slips a wrong link into a document, they can easily articulate the exact physical mistake or misstep they made when asked.
* **Mixed Registers and Tone:** Humans fluidly balance formal and casual language, integrate slang, utilize humor, or display uneven prose formatting across paragraphs based on shifting moods, energy levels, or editing sessions.
* **Idiosyncratic or Niche Errors:** Errors made in specialized formatting environments (like visual text editors) leave disorganized, messy trails that differ sharply from the highly uniform, predictable formatting mistakes generated by an AI model.
